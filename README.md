# Courtroom Counsel

Build a complete, polished, visually impressive web application called THE COURTROOM.

This is a two-person college project, so make it look like a professionally designed real product rather than a basic student website. It must be fully functional, interactive, responsive, visually rich, and entertaining.

CORE CONCEPT

THE COURTROOM is a digital courtroom where users submit any everyday problem, dilemma, or situation.

The website should initially appear to genuinely understand and analyze the user's problem. It should identify what the problem is about, ask several unique questions specifically related to that exact problem, consider the user's answers, and then appear to prepare a thoughtful solution.

But there is one major flaw:

THE FINAL VERDICT IS COMPLETELY UNRELATED.

The verdict should be absurd, useless, unexpected, and mildly sarcastic.

The comedy comes from this progression:

REAL PROBLEM → RELEVANT QUESTIONS → SERIOUS ANALYSIS → SUSPENSE → COMPLETELY USELESS VERDICT

Do not reveal the joke on the landing page.

4. DYNAMIC PROBLEM-SPECIFIC QUESTION SYSTEM

THIS IS THE MOST IMPORTANT FUNCTIONAL REQUIREMENT.

DO NOT use the same questions for every problem.

The system must first analyze the user's actual input and determine what their problem is about.

Then create/select 4–7 questions that are genuinely relevant to that specific problem.

Different problems should produce noticeably different questions.

Examples:

USER PROBLEM:

“I keep procrastinating when studying.”

Possible questions:

- What type of study tasks do you procrastinate on most?

- When do you usually find yourself avoiding studying?

- What normally distracts you when you try to begin?

- Is the main difficulty starting, understanding the material, or staying focused?

- Do deadlines change how easily you get started?

- What have you already tried to overcome it?

---

USER PROBLEM:

“My friend keeps ignoring my messages.”

Possible questions:

- How long has your friend been responding differently?

- Did anything happen between you before this started?

- Do they ignore everyone or mainly your messages?

- How frequently did you communicate before?

- Have you tried asking them directly about it?

- What kind of response are you hoping to receive?

---

USER PROBLEM:

“I keep losing my keys.”

Possible questions:

- Where do you normally put your keys when you get home?

- How often do you realize they are missing?

- Where do you usually find them afterward?

- Do you have a specific place where you normally keep them?

- Do you frequently misplace other belongings too?

---

USER PROBLEM:

“I don't know which college to choose.”

Possible questions:

- What are your most important priorities when choosing a college?

- Which courses or career paths are you considering?

- How important are location and travel?

- How important are fees and affordability?

- What opportunities are you comparing between the colleges?

- What would you regret most about choosing the wrong one?

---

USER PROBLEM:

“My roommate keeps taking my charger without asking.”

Possible questions:

- How frequently does this happen?

- Do they return the charger afterward?

- Have you already asked them not to take it?

- Is this happening only with your charger or other belongings too?

- Does it interfere with your ability to use your device?

The system should support categories including:

- academics

- procrastination

- time management

- friendships

- social situations

- decision-making

- organization

- motivation

- food

- money

- college

- technology

- habits

- projects

- productivity

- everyday problems

- general/unknown problems

For unexpected problems, create/select broader questions that still relate to the user's actual situation.

---

5. QUESTIONS MUST ALSO BE UNIQUE WITHIN ONE CASE

Do not ask essentially the same question multiple times.

Each question should investigate a different aspect of the problem.

For example, do NOT ask:

“How often does this happen?”

“How frequently does this happen?”

“How many times does this happen?”

Those are basically the same question.

Instead, progressively investigate different aspects.

Where possible, use the user's previous answers to influence the next question.

The questioning should feel like a real interviewer/judge is listening to the case and deciding what information is needed next.

The user should genuinely think:

“Wait… this actually understands my problem.”

9. VERDICT REVEAL

Create a dramatic suspenseful transition.

First show:

AFTER CAREFUL CONSIDERATION…

Pause.

Then:

THE COURT HAS REACHED A DECISION.

Then reveal:

⚖️ FINAL VERDICT

The verdict should appear like an official court decision.

The verdict MUST be:

- completely unrelated to the original problem

- absurd

- useless

- harmless

- unexpected

- different from previous verdicts

Examples:

Problem: “I keep procrastinating.”

VERDICT:

BUY A CABBAGE.

Court order:

“Place it somewhere visible and contemplate its career trajectory.”

Problem: “My friend keeps ignoring my messages.”

VERDICT:

ROTATE YOUR PILLOW 90 DEGREES.

Court order:

“The court sees no reason why your pillow should remain uninvolved.”

Problem: “I keep losing my keys.”

VERDICT:

DRAW A HORSE.

Court order:

“The court expects improvement.”

Problem: “I don't know which college to choose.”

VERDICT:

COUNT EVERY CHAIR IN YOUR HOUSE.

Court order:

“Return to this matter once the census is complete.”

Problem: “I have too much homework.”

VERDICT:

STAND NEXT TO A WINDOW FOR 47 SECONDS.

Court order:

“Observe nothing in particular.”

Do not simply reuse these examples. Create a large bank of safe, absurd, unrelated verdicts and randomize them.

Never generate dangerous, humiliating, discriminatory, sexual, or harmful instructions.

10. SERIOUS COURT REASONING

Under the ridiculous verdict, show:

COURT'S REASONING

Use extremely serious legal-style wording that sounds convincing but doesn't meaningfully connect to the original problem.

Example:

“After careful examination of the testimony, contributing circumstances, and available evidence, the court has determined that the recommended intervention represents the most appropriate course of action at this time.”

Add playful sarcastic lines such as:

“We trust this resolves everything.”

“The court has done its part.”

“Further complaints may be submitted to absolutely no one.”

“The court will not be taking questions at this time.”

“You presented the problem. We provided the solution. What more could you possibly want?”

Keep the sarcasm playful and mildly rude, not genuinely abusive.

12. APPEAL SYSTEM

When the user clicks:

APPEAL THE VERDICT

Display:

APPEAL ACCEPTED

“The original verdict is currently under review.”

Ask 1–2 additional questions that are genuinely relevant to the original problem.

Then show another serious analysis stage.

Finally deliver another completely unrelated verdict.

Do not repeat the previous verdict.

Allow multiple appeals.

The questions are a major part of the project.

“This understands my problem.”
